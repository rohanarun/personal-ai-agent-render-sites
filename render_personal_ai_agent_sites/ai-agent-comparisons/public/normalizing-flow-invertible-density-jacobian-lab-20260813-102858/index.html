Change of variables

Shape changes. Probability mass does not vanish.

Start with a Gaussian whose density is easy to evaluate. Compose reversible transformations until its samples match a target shape. Regions that expand become less dense; regions that contract become more dense. The determinant measures that local volume change.

One flow step

Map. Measure. Reverse.

Choose an invertible map.

Coupling layers transform part of a vector using the rest. Their triangular Jacobians make both inversion and determinant evaluation tractable.

Account for local volume.

The change-of-variables formula adds the log absolute Jacobian determinant to the base log density. Expansion lowers density; contraction raises it.

Run either direction.

Forward evaluation maps data to a simple latent density for likelihood. Reverse sampling draws latent noise and maps it into structured data.

Design tradeoffs

Expressivity

Simple triangular maps are cheap and exact, but many layers may be needed to represent tangled multimodal densities.

Invertibility

A valid flow cannot fold two latent points onto one output. That structural constraint preserves density accounting but limits possible maps.

Jacobian cost

General neural transformations have expensive determinants. Coupling, autoregressive, and residual designs impose structure to keep computation practical.
