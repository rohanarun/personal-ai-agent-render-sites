Three evidence boundaries matter.

What the disclosure establishes

ReelFarm’s founder publicly described 25–30% churn over a period of years and named product changes intended to improve retention.

What it does not establish

The figure alone does not prove fraud, deliberate customer cycling, a universal lack of customer value, or the measurement period.

What would resolve the uncertainty

A precise churn definition, signup-cohort retention, plan-level results, refund data, and controlled sales and profit measurement.

The short version

If 25–30% is monthly customer churn, the retention picture is severe. If it is quarterly or annual, it is materially different. Buyers should not have to guess.

Jump to the buyer checklist

On August 26 and August 29, 2026, ReelFarm founder Matt Welter wrote publicly that ReelFarm had experienced “25–30% churn for YEARS.” He did not define the period, whether it was customer or revenue churn, or whether failed payments were included.

In the August 29 post, Welter listed five product changes intended to improve retention: a new queue and schedule, bulk creation, approval and editing controls, a redesigned user experience, and the planned removal of the credit system. His August 26 post made the same churn disclosure.

That is unusually candid. It is also a serious warning sign for anyone considering an AI-content subscription. But it needs to be described accurately: high churn is not, by itself, proof of fraud.

What the three-month math actually means

If the disclosed figure is monthly customer churn and the rate remains constant, the retention math is severe:

  • At 25% monthly churn, about 42.2% of a starting customer cohort remains after three months and 17.8% remains after six months.
  • At 30% monthly churn, about 34.3% remains after three months and 11.8% remains after six months.

Under a simple monthly geometric-churn model with a constant rate, average tenure before churn is 1/c: four months at 25% and 3.33 months at 30%.

An average tenure of 3.33 months does not mean every customer disappears every 3.33 months. At 30% monthly churn, roughly one-third of the original cohort would still remain after three months. The accurate conclusion is that most of a starting cohort would be gone within that period and continued growth would require substantial replacement acquisition.

If the founder meant quarterly churn, the average tenure would be far longer. If he meant annual churn, longer still. Buyers should not have to guess.

Churn is a retention warning, not a fraud verdict

A 25–30% monthly churn rate can result from very different problems:

  • Customers may buy the product for a short campaign and then leave.
  • The workflow may produce content volume without producing sustained business value.
  • Credit limits, unused credits, editing friction, or inconsistent output may push users to cancel.
  • Customers may have unrealistic expectations about what automated content can accomplish.
  • The product may be improving quickly but failing to bring former customers back.

Those explanations are not interchangeable. Resolving them requires cohort data, not a handful of favorable or unfavorable anecdotes.

The missing evidence is audited sales impact

As reviewed August 30, 2026, ReelFarm’s public site emphasized automated faceless TikTok slideshows, publishing, scheduling, and analytics for views, likes, and shares. It displayed testimonials attributed to customers claiming profit and millions of views. This review did not independently authenticate those testimonials or test the product’s generation and publishing functionality.

Views are not incremental sales. Posts are not profit. Content volume is not product-market fit.

The central buyer question is whether generated content creates more profitable customer behavior than the alternatives after ad spend, subscription cost, account-management time, refunds, and brand risk are included.

ReelFarm’s Terms of Service, reviewed August 30, 2026, state that billing is monthly; generated AI content is non-refundable once created; credits used for generated content cannot be restored regardless of satisfaction; and refunds are limited based on credit usage and payment age. The terms also state that moodboard images come without a warranty of ownership or licensing and place responsibility for public or commercial use on the customer.

Those terms do not prove wrongdoing. They do show that a meaningful portion of performance, content-rights, and refund risk sits with the buyer.

What ReelFarm should publish next

If the retention problem is being fixed, the strongest response would be transparent evidence:

  1. Define the 25–30% figure precisely: period, customer or revenue churn, cohort, and treatment of failed payments.
  2. Publish retention by signup month and plan, not only a blended average.
  3. Separate episodic campaign users from customers who intended to subscribe continuously.
  4. Report conversion and profit outcomes, not only views and engagement.
  5. Compare AI-only creative, AI-assisted real footage, and human-created UGC using the same offer, audience, spend, and attribution window.
  6. Disclose refund rates and the percentage of customers who exhaust, partially use, or abandon credits.
  7. Document the commercial-use rights for every image, avatar, voice, and source asset.

What buyers should do now

Do not purchase an AI-content tool because a founder posts revenue screenshots, because a testimonial reports a viral video, or because the software can generate hundreds of assets.

Run a small controlled test. Pre-register the offer, audience, spend, attribution window, and success metric. Compare the AI output against real customer footage or a human creator. Measure sales, contribution margin, refunds, repeat purchases, and brand complaints. Stop if the tool cannot beat the baseline.

ReelFarm’s disclosed churn is a legitimate reason for skepticism. It suggests a major retention problem if the figure is monthly. It does not, standing alone, prove that ReelFarm is a scam or that it intentionally cycles customers.

The fair, hard conclusion is simpler: buyers deserve the missing definition and outcome data before treating automated AI content as a reliable growth engine.
