Super vs Riverside

Prepare better podcast questions without turning a guest's old bio into today's facts.

For independent podcast producers. Published by Super Powers AI, which is discussed here. Official pages reviewed September 15, 2026; no head-to-head product test or integration test performed.

Different jobs in the same episode

Riverside is the stronger documented fit for production. Its official site describes local separate-track recording, text-based editing, AI-generated clips and show notes, hosting and distribution. Calling it "just a recorder" would be misleading. Features and limits vary by plan. Riverside product site.

Super's proposed role is upstream research. Its public product describes automated research, cloud browsers and sandboxes. A bounded use is assembling public sources into a draft host brief: what the guest has said, where they said it and what remains unclear. That does not establish recording quality, a Riverside connection or automatic publishing. Super product documentation.

Keep Riverside if your pain is editing or delivering episodes. Consider a separate research workflow only if the bottleneck is preparing informed questions. A combined workflow can make sense; replacing an established recording setup solely for research is not justified by the evidence here.

Build the brief before building the question list

  1. Confirm the guest. Start with a producer-approved public profile, not a name-only search. Record pronunciation, current role and biography as items to confirm with the guest. Do not silently merge people with similar names.
  2. Choose a narrow episode promise. "How a small team changed its onboarding process" produces a more useful research task than "everything about this person." Agree on topics and sensitive boundaries before collecting material.
  3. Gather three kinds of evidence. Keep a current official profile, one relevant primary article or talk, and one prior interview. Save URLs, publication dates, observation dates and exact timestamps where available. Distinguish a transcript from your summary.
  4. Turn evidence into open questions. Attribute the premise and invite correction. Do not convert an old title, anecdote or claimed result into a present-tense fact. Highlight contradictions for the host instead of resolving them by guesswork.
  5. Separate the recording handoff. Send the host a concise brief. Confirm consent, technical setup and schedule through the production process. After recording, check quotes and show notes against the actual episode, not the pre-interview research draft.

Prerequisites include the right guest identity, access to the source material, permission to process any nonpublic notes, a reviewer and a time budget. Avoid collecting private contact information when a guest-approved booking channel already exists.

Rewrite three risky questions

Fictional preparation exercise: the guest has a two-year-old company bio, a talk describing one small-team experiment, and an interview with an ambiguous transcript. Open each item for a safer host question.

The old job title

Risky: "As the current head of product, what is your strategy?"

Better: "Your earlier company bio listed you as head of product. How has your role changed since then?"

Still needed: Confirm the current introduction with the guest before recording. An old official page can be authentic and outdated.

The result without a baseline

Risky: "How did you prove your process works for every team?"

Better: "In your talk, you described a small-team trial. What changed, how did you measure it, and what would you hesitate to generalize?"

Still needed: Locate the exact passage and retain the trial's scope. A reported experience is not a universal benchmark.

The uncertain quotation

Risky: "Why did you say you never test ideas?"

Better: "Could you explain how you decide which ideas need testing?"

Still needed: Listen to the original recording before quoting. If the wording remains unclear, ask without attributing the disputed phrase.

Technology, with a freshness boundary

The public computer-use-cache provides inspectable implementation, not merely a product slogan. The pinned source hashes normalized request content, returns eligible stored responses on hits and sends misses upstream. It includes expiry and bypass controls. A streaming miss is not stored.

A returned response is not a fresh browser visit or a completed external action. Metadata is ignored by default; changing only a date there does not reliably request fresh facts. For an introduction that must be current, bring fresh source content or bypass reuse, then have a person confirm it. No cache hit rate, preparation-time saving or cost reduction was measured for this use case.

Compare the bill, not the slogan

Riverside's pricing page lists free and paid offerings, with limits around separate-track downloads and plan-specific features. The retrieved page contained a staging warning and conflicting price text, so this article deliberately does not quote a reliable current paid price. Confirm the selected billing term, download allowance, editor seats and any AI-credit charges in the live checkout before purchase.

Super describes wallet-based pay-as-you-go use and optional plans. A research task is not priced like a recording seat; we have not established a fixed cost per guest brief. Pilot one episode, record actual charges and review time, and compare against your existing preparation method.

A useful first-episode test

Give the same approved source set to your existing process and the proposed Super workflow. Check whether each factual premise is traceable, whether dates survive summarization and whether uncertain quotations stay uncertain. Count corrections the host must make. Then assess recording and editing separately in the production tool. Do not use a tidy-looking brief as proof of factual accuracy.
