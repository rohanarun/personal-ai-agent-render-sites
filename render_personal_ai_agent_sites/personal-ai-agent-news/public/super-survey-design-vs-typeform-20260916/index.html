A survey brief that earns its questions

Imagine a team considering a new onboarding guide. “Would you love a simpler onboarding experience?” makes agreement easy but does not identify a concrete failure. Start with a decision: which part of setup should the team investigate next?

  1. Define the audience and recall window. For example, people who attempted setup in the past seven days. Someone who never tried it cannot report that experience.
  2. Separate behavior from preference. Ask what happened during their latest attempt before asking what they might want. Hypothetical enthusiasm is not observed adoption.
  3. Make each answer interpretable. A rating for both speed and clarity cannot tell you which dimension needs work.
  4. Pilot the whole sequence. Ask a few intended respondents to explain what they think each question means. Record confusion and revise. This checks comprehension; a small convenience pilot does not establish population demand.
  5. Approve, then collect. Check response choices, branching and the final mobile form. Keep a versioned question list so later edits do not silently change the measure.

Pew Research Center explains why specific wording, one concept per question, and nonoverlapping response categories matter. It also notes that question order can influence responses. These principles inform the examples below; they do not make them validated instruments. Survey wording guidance.

What to ask Super, and what not to delegate
A concrete preparation request might be:
Using only these approved notes, draft questions about the latest setup attempt for people who tried it within seven days. Separate distinct concepts, flag assumptions, propose comprehension probes, and list what evidence each answer could and could not support. Do not contact customers or publish the survey.
Supply the decision, eligible audience, known terminology, and permitted source material. Review every question with someone who understands the customers. Keep identifiable responses out of a general drafting request unless your organization's privacy process permits that use. Neither an assistant's confidence nor a clean export replaces participant consent or research governance.

Real cache technology, bounded claims
Super's public Computer-Use Cache repository provides inspectable implementation, not only a marketing claim. A hit can avoid another upstream model request. It does not prove a browser page is current, that a form was submitted, or that a survey is valid. The practical recommendation
Keep Typeform when response collection is the bottleneck. Evaluate Super when assembling source-grounded preparation is the bottleneck. Use both only if the handoff actually saves your team work; this article has not measured that saving.
Before paying, compare your expected respondent volume and collaborators against Typeform's current limits, and test a bounded preparation task against Super's current wallet charges. These are different units of work, so a simple subscription-versus-agent price ranking would mislead.
