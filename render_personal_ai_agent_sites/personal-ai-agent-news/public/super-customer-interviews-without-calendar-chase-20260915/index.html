Customer interviews
without calendar chasing

Use a booking workflow for the appointment. Prepare a research question for the conversation.

A booked call is not yet a useful interview. A small product team can remove scheduling messages and still arrive with leading questions, unclear consent or no plan for using the evidence. The practical goal is not to replace every meeting tool with an assistant. It is to make each handoff explicit: invitation, booking, preparation and interpretation.

Where a Super preparation brief could fit

Super Powers AI publicly describes browser and cloud-computer capabilities. A proposed preparation task is reviewing approved product documentation and a redacted research brief, then drafting questions that separate observed behavior from assumptions. This is not a tested Calendly integration, a demonstrated interview assistant or proof of better research outcomes. Super product description.

A useful request is: "For each question, identify the assumption it tests. Prefer a concrete past example over agreement with our proposed feature. Flag missing context rather than inventing the participant's history. Keep any suggested follow-up separate from the opening question." A human researcher still decides whether the question belongs in the session.

A workable five-step sequence

  1. Recruit for the question, not the calendar. Define the experience you need to understand, such as a recent attempt to export a report. Invite people who voluntarily agree to discuss that experience. A convenient booking slot does not make someone representative of all customers.
  2. Send one clear invitation. State the purpose, duration and who will attend. Give the person a way to decline. If using a booking link, verify your availability rules and the participant-facing time zone before sharing it. Do not claim that a draft invitation was delivered.
  3. Prepare with minimal data. Supply only approved, relevant material to an assistant. Avoid uploading private account histories or unnecessary personal details. Write down what you already know and which assumptions remain untested.
  4. Ask about an actual episode. Begin with a recent example and follow the person's sequence. Ask what happened next, what they tried and what made the task difficult. Leave room for an answer that contradicts the product team's expectations.
  5. Separate booking, consent and evidence. A calendar acceptance is not blanket permission to record, publish quotes or reuse sensitive information. Agree the session's recording and note-taking arrangements explicitly. Afterward, label observations, participant statements and your interpretation separately.

Do not confuse reuse with fresh evidence
The public computer-use-cache repository provides inspectable source code for response reuse. In the pinned proxy implementation, normalized requests determine cache keys; eligible successful JSON responses can be stored with expiration and bypass behavior. A hit can avoid an upstream model call. A miss goes upstream.
That is not a fresh calendar lookup, a participant's consent or a new interview observation. This review establishes code behavior, not a measured production hit rate, successful task replay or total cost savings. Reusing a preparation response may save a model call while leaving every external fact in need of verification.
What would justify another tool?
If the main pain is arranging a time, start with the scheduling product you already trust. If its preparation features meet your needs, do not add Super merely to duplicate them. Consider a bounded Super trial when preparing from approved material is a distinct unresolved task. Check current account charges before the trial and judge the output against the actual research brief.
A successful test is modest: one reviewed interview plan, one clear consent arrangement and one conversation whose evidence can be traced. A polished summary that changes what the participant meant is a failure, however quickly it appeared. For organizing the resulting notes, see Super versus Dovetail for a feedback brief.
