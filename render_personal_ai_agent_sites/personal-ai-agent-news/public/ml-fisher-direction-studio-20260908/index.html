A supervised direction

Fisher's two-class criterion rewards projected mean separation relative to scatter within each class. For a direction w and class mean difference delta, this lab uses J(w) = (w-transpose delta) squared / (w-transpose Sw w). Sw is the sum of centered outer products within both classes, with no division by sample count.

When Sw is invertible, an optimal direction is proportional to Sw-inverse delta. The lab solves Sw w = delta with QR decomposition from ml-matrix and normalizes w to unit length. Flipping the sign of w changes the coordinate orientation, but not J.

Why the direction tilts

The eight synthetic points contain four symmetric offsets around each class mean. Their means lie on the horizontal axis. Rotating an elongated within-class cloud can make horizontal projection noisy, even when it preserves the entire mean gap. A tilted direction can sacrifice some gap to reduce within-class scatter more strongly.

Spread ratio scales the long offset relative to the fixed short offset of 0.4. Both classes share this geometry. Ratio one makes the within-class scatter isotropic; Fisher's direction then agrees with the horizontal mean difference. These are designed observations, not independent Gaussian samples.

What this score cannot tell you

J is a separation criterion for this labeled dataset. It is not accuracy, a probability, or a margin guarantee. A projection learned from labels must be fit inside the training split before evaluating held-out data. A good in-sample direction can still generalize poorly.

Probabilistic linear discriminant analysis adds modeling assumptions, commonly Gaussian class distributions with shared covariance, to produce classification rules. This studio only demonstrates the projection objective. It uses no class priors, decision threshold, regularization, or held-out evaluation. The chosen nonzero offsets keep Sw full rank throughout the controls.
