Small edit, large spectral move

Both unperturbed matrices have eigenvalues 1 and 1. Equal-sized changes to their entries can produce very different movements of those eigenvalues.

The perturbations have equal spectral norm epsilon, but different entry patterns. This is a controlled example, not an exhaustive worst-case search.

The characteristic polynomial tells the story
J = [[1, k], [0, 1]], E = [[0, 0], [s epsilon, 0]]
det(lambda I - J - E) = (lambda - 1)2 - s k epsilon
With positive sign s, the roots are 1 plus or minus sqrt(k epsilon). With negative sign they are 1 plus or minus i sqrt(k epsilon). Their distance from 1 is the same. At epsilon = 0 and k > 0, J has only one independent eigenvector: its repeated eigenvalue is defective.
N = I, F = [[0, s epsilon], [s epsilon, 0]]
eigenvalues of N + F: 1 - epsilon and 1 + epsilon
The reference is symmetric and its displacement is exactly epsilon. Both E and F have spectral norm epsilon. The Jordan displacement is sqrt(k epsilon), so displacement divided by perturbation is sqrt(k/epsilon). Smaller perturbations give smaller absolute movements but a larger amplification ratio.
Sensitivity is not solver error
The two eigenvalue sets are computed with ml-matrix and agree with the closed-form roots. A large change after altering the input matrix can be a property of the problem, not a failure of the eigensolver. This example does not imply that every nonsymmetric matrix is badly conditioned, or that every perturbation of this Jordan matrix has the displayed effect.
Both axes of the sweep are logarithmic, with epsilon from 10-6 to 10-1. Jordan displacement has log-log slope 1/2; the symmetric reference has slope 1. The green circle in the complex plane has radius epsilon. The sweep holds k fixed and uses the selected perturbation sign.
