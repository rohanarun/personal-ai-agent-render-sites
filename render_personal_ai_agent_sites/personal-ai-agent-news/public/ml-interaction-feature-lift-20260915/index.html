A New Feature, Not a New Straight Line

What changes when a classifier can look at the product of two inputs, not just each input separately?

Lift a parity-shaped dataset into three dimensions. Learn why no line separates the original classes, how one interaction feature creates a separating plane, and why that plane is nonlinear when viewed in the original input space.

Why the original line cannot work

Focus on four inputs: (1,1) and (−1,−1) are positive; (1,−1) and (−1,1) are negative. This is the familiar XOR separability problem with the class names swapped. Each class occupies a different pair of opposite corners.

Suppose an affine score g(x)=a₁x₁+a₂x₂+c strictly separates them. Averaging g over the two positive corners gives c, which would have to be positive. Averaging over the two negative corners also gives c, which would have to be negative. That contradiction rules out every affine separator, not just an unfortunate line you happened to try.

Equivalently, the line segment joining each same-class pair passes through the origin. Their convex hulls overlap. Rotating or translating a straight boundary cannot fix this geometry. Our 36-point dataset contains the same pattern at several magnitudes, so the obstruction is still present even though the exact corner (1,1) is used only in this proof.

Let the representation express an interaction

Add one feature and keep the original two: φλ(x)=(x₁,x₂,λx₁x₂). Same-sign inputs have positive products; opposite-sign inputs have negative products. When λ is positive, lifting places the two classes on opposite sides of z=0. The horizontal plane now separates them.

Our classifier has fixed feature-space weight w=(0,0,1) and score wᵀφλ(x)−b = λx₁x₂−b. It predicts positive only when that score is strictly greater than zero. We do not train the weights or search for a separator. The feature was deliberately chosen because we know the label rule; the experiment demonstrates representational possibility, not successful feature discovery.

The wireframe is a saddle because the product rises in two quadrants and falls in the others. The 3D view matters here: it shows how points inseparable in the ground plane acquire a new coordinate that a plane can use. Nothing about their first two coordinates changes.

Check the numbers, not just the picture

The dataset is the Cartesian product of {−1.2,−0.8,−0.4,0.4,0.8,1.2} with itself. It has 18 positive and 18 negative points. At λ=0 and b=0, every score is zero. The stated tie rule predicts negative, so accuracy is 18/36. That number evaluates this fixed rule; it is not the best accuracy obtainable by every possible affine classifier.

At λ=1 and b=0, all 36 predictions are correct. The smallest absolute product is 0.4×0.4=0.16. Since the feature-space normal w has unit norm, the minimum signed distance to this particular plane is 0.16. At λ=0.5 it is 0.08, even though the predictions are unchanged.

Move b to 0.30 with full lift. Two positive points have product 0.16 and now fall below the plane. All other points remain correct, giving 34/36. The minimum signed margin becomes −0.14. A negative signed margin means at least one point lies on the wrong side; it is not a probability.

The probe provides a separate input, not an extra training point. At reset coordinates (0.8,0.6), full lift gives z=0.48. With b=0.30 its score is 0.18, so it predicts positive. Move either coordinate across zero and observe which interactions change sign.

A plane becomes a nonlinear boundary

The feature-space boundary is simply z=b. Substituting the feature map gives λx₁x₂=b in the original plane. At positive λ and b=0, that boundary is the union of the two coordinate axes. For nonzero b, it is a hyperbola. A linear classifier in transformed coordinates can therefore define a nonlinear decision rule in the original inputs.

At λ=0, the interaction coordinate vanishes. If b=0, all inputs are on the boundary; if b is nonzero, the fixed classifier predicts one class everywhere. Dividing by λ in this case would be invalid. The interface computes the score directly, so the zero-lift case has no artificial singularity.

The link to kernels

Inner products in this explicit feature space obey φλ(x)ᵀφλ(u)=xᵀu+λ²(x₁x₂)(u₁u₂). This defines a valid kernel because it is an ordinary inner product after a feature map. Notice λ is squared: scaling a feature changes the geometry of pairwise inner products quadratically.

This is a particular interaction kernel, not the complete degree-two polynomial kernel, which includes additional terms. A compatible kernel method can use these inner products without explicitly constructing every feature coordinate. The page constructs them explicitly so that you can inspect them; it does not run an SVM or claim that kernels automatically choose the right representation.

What the clean separation does not establish

Perfect fit here is built into the construction: labels are the sign of the product and the feature contains that product. Real labels can be noisy, useful interactions can be unknown, and additional features can increase overfitting. Generalization must be checked on data not used to choose the representation or tune the decision rule.

The displayed margin is measured in our chosen feature coordinates. Increasing λ stretches one axis and increases that margin without making the underlying task intrinsically easier. Comparing raw margins across differently scaled feature maps is not a generalization guarantee. The useful lesson is the change in what a linear rule can express, not a claim that a taller plot proves a better model.
