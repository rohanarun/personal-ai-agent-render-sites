Median Slope

One unusual observation can rotate a least-squares line. A median of pairwise slopes responds differently. Move the last point in this seven-observation synthetic dataset.

Green solid line = least squares. Magenta dashed line = Theil-Sen. The orange point at x = 6 is editable. Axes stay fixed while it moves.

Twenty-one slopes, one median

Seven distinct x values produce 7 times 6 divided by 2 = 21 pairs. Each pair contributes (y[j] - y[i]) / (x[j] - x[i]). The eleventh sorted slope is the median, highlighted below.

The intercept convention matters

This demo uses the joint convention: median of y[i] minus slope times x[i]. Other intercept conventions exist. The least-squares comparison instead minimizes summed squared vertical residuals with an intercept.

Why one point has limited influence here

Moving the last point changes six of the 21 slopes; the other 15 stay fixed. The median can still move, but the extreme slopes need not become the middle slope. This is a specific seven-point example, not a universal immunity guarantee.

Robust is not automatically better

Neither line establishes causality or predicts held-out performance. An unusual point can be valid evidence rather than a measurement error. Multiple contaminated observations, a nonlinear relationship, or a poorly chosen feature can undermine the fit. No uncertainty interval is computed here.
