Centered Gram spectrum

From distances to coordinates

Classical multidimensional scaling starts with pairwise dissimilarities, not point coordinates. It squares those distances, double-centers them, and multiplies by -1/2 to form a centered Gram matrix B. For Euclidean distances this matrix encodes inner products.

The eigendecomposition of B supplies axes. Each retained positive eigenvector is scaled by the square root of its eigenvalue. This studio uses ml-matrix and the largest one or two positive eigenvalues, with missing axes filled by zero.

Two different kinds of mismatch

A tetrahedron has Euclidean distances but needs three positive dimensions. A two-dimensional map must lose information. Substantial negative eigenvalues indicate a different problem: the dissimilarities cannot be reproduced exactly by Euclidean points in any dimension. The inconsistent preset violates a triangle inequality.

Near-zero eigenvalues are numerical noise: this example uses tolerance 1e-9 times the larger of 1 and the largest absolute eigenvalue. The positive dimension count uses the same tolerance. The all-zero matrix represents four coincident points, not a numerical failure.

A useful map is not a proof

MDS is a dimensionality-reduction and visualization method. Classical MDS fits the centered Gram structure; it does not directly minimize the displayed pair-distance RMSE. That diagnostic averages squared errors over the six unordered pairs. Rotations, reflections, and repeated-eigenvalue choices can change the picture without changing its distance fit.

A visually separated group is not automatically a class or a causal explanation. Inspect individual errors before interpreting apparent neighborhoods. These are synthetic distances without labels or a learned model; the export preserves the original table, spectrum, and reconstruction for comparison.
