How stable is the leading axis?

Two covariance eigenvalues can be close even when the matrix looks well behaved. Change a symmetric perturbation and compare the original and updated principal directions, without confusing an axis with its sign.

Both matrices remain positive definite throughout these controls. Ellipse radii are square roots of eigenvalues, not confidence intervals or sampled data.

The gap protects a direction
A is diagonal with entries 1 + g and 1. The perturbation is t times [[cos(2 phi), sin(2 phi)], [sin(2 phi), -cos(2 phi)]], so its spectral norm is t. Its orientation changes while its size stays fixed. The updated eigengap is sqrt((g + 2t cos(2 phi)) squared + (2t sin(2 phi)) squared).
When either leading eigenvalue is tied, a single leading axis is not uniquely defined. The display withholds the angle and bound instead of comparing arbitrary eigenvectors. At orientation 90 degrees, increasing t past g/2 crosses a tie and swaps the leading axis by 90 degrees.
A bound with its denominator visible
Let v be the unit leading eigenvector of A + E with eigenvalue lambda. Projecting its eigenvalue equation onto the second coordinate gives (lambda - 1) v[2] = (Ev)[2]. If lambda - 1 is positive, sin(theta) is at most t / (lambda - 1). This example-specific bound compares the updated leading eigenvalue to the original lower eigenvalue; it is not an unconditional t/g rule.
Eigenvector perturbation theory connects changes in eigenspaces to spectral separation. In PCA, a nearly tied pair of variances can make an individual component unstable even when the two-dimensional space is unchanged. These constructed covariances illustrate that distinction; no dataset or statistical uncertainty estimate is implied.
Eldridge, Belkin and Wang: Unperturbed (ALT 2018) discusses classical eigenvector perturbation bounds. The two-dimensional construction and projected-equation bound above are worked directly for this experiment.
