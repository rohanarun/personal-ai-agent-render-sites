Orthogonal in which geometry?

The generalized eigenproblem is A v = lambda B v. With symmetric A and positive definite B, its eigenvectors can be normalized so that V transpose B V = I. This is the type-1 normalization in the SciPy generalized symmetric eigenproblem documentation. Their ordinary Euclidean dot product need not be zero.

C = B^(-1/2) A B^(-1/2), y = B^(1/2) x

In these transformed coordinates, the quotient becomes y transpose C y / y transpose y. Expanding y in an orthonormal eigenbasis of C makes this a weighted average of its eigenvalues, with nonnegative weights summing to one. That proves the minimum and maximum bounds, and explains why the eigenvector directions attain them.

Changing B changes the question: the same output is divided by a different directional cost. Identity B recovers the ordinary Rayleigh quotient. A scalar multiple of a direction changes neither quotient; eigenvector signs are also arbitrary.

This two-dimensional example illustrates generalized symmetric eigenproblems used in quadratic optimization. It is not a trained model or a claim that every nonsymmetric or indefinite eigenproblem has these properties. The angle curve is sampled every degree; the eigenvalue bounds are computed from the matrix, not estimated from that grid.
