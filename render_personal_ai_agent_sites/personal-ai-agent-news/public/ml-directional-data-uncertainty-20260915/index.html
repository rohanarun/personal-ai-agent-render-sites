More Data, Same Blind Spot

Why can forty observations leave one prediction just as uncertain as it was before you collected any data?

In Bayesian regression, the direction of a measurement matters as much as its count. Learn to read a posterior, distinguish parameter uncertainty from observation noise, and identify which next measurement would answer your actual question.

One equation does not identify two weights

Suppose a response is a weighted combination of two features: y = h₁w₁ + h₂w₂ + ε. Each feature vector h selects one direction in the weight plane. Measuring the sum of two weights precisely does not tell you their difference. Many pairs have the same sum. More repetitions can refine that sum without distinguishing those pairs.

The first measurement here has unit direction h = (1,1)/√2. A perpendicular query uses q = (−1,1)/√2. Their dot product is zero. If you only observe h, the likelihood never distinguishes weights that differ along q. The long direction of the green posterior is that remaining ambiguity. It is not a numerical optimization failure.

A fully specified inference problem

We use a standard normal prior w ~ N(0,I) and independent Gaussian observation noise with variance σ² = 0.25. The two measurement groups have unit feature vectors separated by your chosen angle. Each group contains the selected number of independent measurements in the assumed model.

To isolate geometry, the displayed dataset is constructed: every response in a group equals hᵀ(1,0). No random observations are drawn and no model is trained. Equal responses are an intentional illustration, not evidence that real noise vanishes. The assumed noise variance stays 0.25. Counts mean additional independent evidence, never duplicate copies of the same observation.

Completing the square in the Gaussian prior times the Gaussian likelihood gives a Gaussian posterior. Its precision P, covariance C and mean m are:

P = I + 4n₁h₁h₁ᵀ + 4n₂h₂h₂ᵀ
C = P⁻¹
m = C · 4(n₁y₁h₁ + n₂y₂h₂)

Each outer product adds precision along a measured direction. The identity term comes from the prior and keeps P invertible even when every observation points the same way. Inversion and eigendecomposition use the ml-matrix numerical library. This is exact finite-dimensional Gaussian inference up to floating-point arithmetic.

Three experiments worth comparing

Repetition: keep the second count at zero. With four first observations, variance along h is 1/17, while perpendicular variance is 1. With forty, those values are 1/161 and still 1. The standard deviation along the pink perpendicular probe remains exactly 1. A narrower ridge is not a uniformly better understood model.

Coverage: set both counts to four and separation to 90°. The two measurement directions span the plane equally. Now C = I/17: every unit probe has standard deviation about 0.2425. The posterior mean is (16/17,0), pulled slightly toward the prior's zero mean. Rotate the probe and verify that its uncertainty no longer depends on angle.

Near redundancy: keep both counts at four but reduce separation toward zero. Eight measurements of almost the same combination leave a poorly measured direction. At zero separation, variance is 1/33 along that combination and 1 perpendicular to it. The total count is unchanged; the information geometry is not.

A prediction has its own uncertainty

For a unit query q, the unknown noiseless response f = qᵀw has posterior mean qᵀm and variance qᵀCq. Its central 95% interval uses approximately 1.96 standard deviations on either side of the mean. The interface computes this interval for the pink direction, rather than treating a single parameter error bar as universal.

A new noisy observation has variance qᵀCq + 0.25. Even if weight uncertainty becomes tiny, an individual observation still contains noise. At the balanced four-plus-four setting, latent standard deviation is about 0.2425, but observation standard deviation is about 0.5557. More evidence about weights cannot remove that assumed independent noise floor.

The ground ellipse encloses 95% of the joint two-dimensional posterior using radius √5.9915 in whitened coordinates. It is not made by putting 1.96 on both axes. The bell surface is rescaled to height one at its peak so thin posteriors remain inspectable; its volume is therefore not one. Only its shape and contours describe relative density.

What this suggests, and what it does not prove

When choosing data, ask which predictions matter and whether your measurements constrain their directions. Under this model, an observation along h changes covariance to C − ChhᵀC/(σ² + hᵀCh). The reduction in a query's variance is (qᵀCh)²/(σ² + hᵀCh). A useful measurement is connected to the query through current covariance, not merely far away in an arbitrary plot.

These conclusions depend on a correct linear likelihood, independent noise, fixed feature scale and fixed prior. Correlated repetitions provide less information than this count model assumes. Misspecified features, unknown noise or distribution shift can make credible intervals misleading. Bayesian uncertainty is conditional on assumptions; this toy is not a calibration benchmark for neural networks or a guarantee about a deployed agent.
