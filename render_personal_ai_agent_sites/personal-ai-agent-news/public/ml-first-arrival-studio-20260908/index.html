Arrival is not recurrence

Starting at the target gives an arrival time of zero. A return requires at least one step, even if that step is a self-loop. Its mean is the reciprocal of the target's stationary probability. Arrival from another state depends on the path and its waiting times, not just the target's occupancy.

For this directed cycle, A to C must first advance out of A and then out of B. Each geometric waiting time has mean one divided by its advance probability, so the total is 1/a + 1/b. The C advance rate cannot affect this first arrival, but it does change long-run occupancy and return time.

The part beyond the horizon

Let T be the first-arrival step. The green curve is S(n) = Pr(T > n), computed by removing the target from the transition matrix and evolving the remaining probability mass. The displayed survival sum is S(0) + ... + S(N-1), which equals E[min(T,N)]. It is a lower bound on E[T], not a finite-horizon estimate of the full mean. The unshown tail beyond N can matter when advances are rare.

Two equivalent calculations

For a fixed target, Q is the transition matrix restricted to the other states. The expected arrival vector solves (I - Q)h = 1. We also solve for the stationary distribution pi, form W with every row equal to pi, and compute Z = (I - P + W) inverse. The all-pairs entry is (Z[j,j] - Z[i,j]) / pi[j]. Both calculations agree here.

Positive advance and self-loop probabilities keep this finite chain irreducible and aperiodic. The explorer excludes zero advance rates, which can make a target unreachable; it does not silently replace infinite arrival times with finite values.

Grinstead and Snell, Introduction to Probability, section 11.5 develops first passage, recurrence, and the fundamental-matrix identity. The directed cycle and horizon comparison are worked examples.
