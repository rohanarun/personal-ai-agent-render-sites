Same marginals, different joint policy

Compare all six assignments equally with just the three cyclic assignments equally. Both give every cell probability 1/3. But identity has probability 1/6 in the first policy and 1/3 in the second. Marginals cannot recover these joint probabilities.

Drawing a whole permutation enforces exclusivity. Drawing each agent's task independently from the rows of D does not. The exact probability of no collision under independence is the sum, over all six permutations, of the three selected cell probabilities multiplied together. With uniform marginals this is 6/27, so collision probability is 7/9. These are exact probabilities, not estimates from the draw button.

Why every mixture stays feasible

A permutation matrix has one unit entry in each row and column. In D = sum of p[k] P[k], nonnegative probabilities summing to one preserve those totals. The Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem also supplies the converse: every square doubly stochastic matrix admits such a mixture. This editor constructs mixtures; it does not solve for a decomposition of an arbitrary input matrix.

For multi-agent task allocation, D can describe fractional assignment frequencies while the mixture describes a feasible joint execution. This small example assumes every agent can do every task and tasks have unit capacity. It does not optimize utility, enforce additional scheduling constraints, or prove fairness beyond the displayed marginals.

Manjunath Krishnapur, Topics in Analysis, Theorem 8 (printed pages 52-53) gives the doubly stochastic definition and the permutation-mixture theorem.
