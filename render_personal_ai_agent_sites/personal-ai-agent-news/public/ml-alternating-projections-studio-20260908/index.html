One projection at a time

An orthogonal projection chooses the closest point on a line. This lab alternates two such operations, starting with the selected line. One slider step is one projection, not a two-projection cycle. The first step can start off both lines; every later state lies on the most recently visited line.

Alternating projections is a building block for feasibility methods in optimization. For two closed subspaces, repeated orthogonal projections converge to the projection of the initial point onto their intersection. This two-dimensional example makes that geometry visible; it does not simulate arbitrary nonconvex constraints or a neural-network training run.

Nearly parallel means slow

For distinct lines in this lab, the intersection is the origin. After the first projection, each additional projection multiplies the norm by cos(angle). A full cycle therefore multiplies it by cos(angle) squared. Small positive angles give factors close to one, so many steps can produce little visible progress.

At 90 degrees the origin is reached in at most two projections, up to rounding. At exactly zero degrees the two lines coincide: the intersection becomes the entire horizontal line. The limiting point is then (start x, 0), not necessarily the origin. That change in the target explains why zero degrees is not merely a slower version of one degree.

Track feasibility separately

Distance to A is the absolute y coordinate. Distance to B is the absolute component perpendicular to its direction. A state on one line may still violate the other. The table's distance uses the intersection, while the paired distances show each constraint separately.

The projection matrices are outer products of unit direction vectors, applied with ml-matrix multiplication. Export includes both matrices and all 40 projection steps, independent of playback position. Floating-point values close to zero may retain tiny residuals; this is not a guarantee of finite-step convergence at general angles.
