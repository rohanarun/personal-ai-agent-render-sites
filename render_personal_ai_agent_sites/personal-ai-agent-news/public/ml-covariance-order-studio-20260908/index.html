A matrix inequality tests all directions

B is greater than or equal to A in the positive-semidefinite order when B - A is positive semidefinite. Equivalently, u transpose (B - A) u is nonnegative for every direction u. For covariance matrices, u transpose B u is the variance of the scalar projection along u.

If the difference has both positive and negative eigenvalues, neither matrix dominates the other. A negative-eigenvalue direction witnesses a decrease; a positive-eigenvalue direction witnesses an increase. Equality in some directions is allowed in the nonstrict order.

The default counterexample

At the initial settings, B = [[1.2, 0.4], [0.4, 1.2]]. Its diagonal entries and trace exceed those of I. But along the 135-degree direction its variance is 0.8, below A's 1. Along 45 degrees it is 1.6. The matrices are therefore incomparable.

A controlled family, with an exact answer

B is a rotated diagonal matrix with eigenvalues 1+d+k and 1+d-k. The controls keep both nonnegative, so B remains a valid covariance, including a singular boundary. The difference eigenvalues are d+k and d-k. Thus B dominates when d is at least k, A dominates when d is at most -k, and otherwise neither dominates. At d=k=0 the matrices are equal.

Rotating the axes changes the witness directions without changing the ordering. The trace difference is 2d, so its sign alone cannot detect opposite-signed directional changes. The probe curve follows 1+d+k cos(2 times [probe angle minus axis angle]).

This comparison concerns synthetic second moments only. It says nothing about means, distribution shape, prediction quality, or whether more variance is desirable. The shift control changes the mean of the two eigenvalues, not the random variable's mean.
