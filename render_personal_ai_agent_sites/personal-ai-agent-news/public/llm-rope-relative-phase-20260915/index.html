Start with an invariant
Here the unrotated query is q = (1, 0), and the unrotated key is k = (1/2, sqrt(3)/2). Both have length one, with an initial angle of 60 degrees. Each position contributes 30 degrees of rotation: theta = pi/6 radians per position. The third displayed coordinate is zero; the orbitable scene is a view of this two-dimensional block, not a third embedding feature.
The query rotates by (m+s)theta and the key by (n+s)theta. Their individual directions change when s changes. Their angle difference does not, because both gained the same angle. The unscaled dot product is therefore unchanged, up to floating-point error. The numerical output comes from Three.js vector rotations and a dot product, not a table of preset answers.
Why absolute rotations yield a relative score
Let R(a) be the ordinary counterclockwise rotation matrix. Orthogonality gives R(a)^T = R(-a), while composing rotations adds their angles. For any fixed vectors q and k:
[R(m theta)q]^T [R(n theta)k]
= q^T R((n-m)theta) k.
The common shift cancels for the same reason. With this experiment's unit vectors and initial angle beta = pi/3, the scalar result reduces to:
score = cos(beta + (n-m)theta).
The sign n-m follows our convention: query at m, key at n, both rotated counterclockwise. Reversing the convention without adjusting the formula would reverse the relative phase. This elementary block is the mechanism behind the relative-position dependence described in RoFormer, the original RoPE paper. Higher-dimensional RoPE applies rotations to coordinate pairs with a frequency schedule.
Three checks worth making
  1. Same position is not necessarily perfect alignment. Set m=n=0. The score is 0.5, not 1, because the content vectors already differ by 60 degrees. Position equality does not make different content identical.
  2. A common shift changes the drawing, not the score. Reset to m=0, n=2. The score is -0.5. Move s from 0 to 5: both arrows rotate by 150 degrees, while n-m stays 2 and the score stays -0.5.
  3. A larger distance can recover a high score. The alignment preset uses m=0, n=10. The initial 60 degrees plus 300 degrees of relative rotation makes a full turn, so the score is 1. Increasing n from 10 to 22 adds another full turn and gives the same result.
What one frequency cannot tell you
At theta=pi/6, the phase repeats every 12 integer positions. This block cannot distinguish offsets that differ by 12 when the content vectors are fixed. A decreasing attention score with every additional position is not a property of this individual cosine. Multiple frequencies change the combined behavior; this small experiment is not a context-length guarantee or a reproduction of any trained model.
The common-shift identity is conditional on holding q and k fixed. In a real network, hidden states can change when the surrounding text changes. Causal masks, other tokens, scaling and softmax also matter. We show one unscaled query-key score, which may be negative. It is not an attention probability, and no next-token prediction is being computed.
Rotation preserves length as well as the pair's common-shift dot product. If a test changes the norm while claiming to perform only this rotation, inspect the implementation. Conversely, a correct norm alone does not prove the phase is correct: a sign error also preserves length. Check both the vector coordinates and the relative-score formula.
From geometry to implementation checks
A practical unit test fixes random q and k, compares their rotated dot product with q^T R((n-m)theta)k, and repeats after a shared offset. Test more than equal positions: that special case can hide a sign error. Use tolerances appropriate to the numerical precision rather than requiring exact bit equality.
