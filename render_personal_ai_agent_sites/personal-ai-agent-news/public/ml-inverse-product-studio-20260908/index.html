A⁻¹ undoes A, but the directional averages xᵀAx and xᵀA⁻¹x are not generally reciprocals. Their product measures this mismatch.

Between one and a ceiling

For a unit x and symmetric positive definite A with eigenvalues in [m, M], the product P = (xᵀAx)(xᵀA⁻¹x) satisfies 1 ≤ P ≤ (M + m)²/(4Mm). The upper limit is Kantorovich’s inequality. The lower limit follows from Cauchy–Schwarz applied to A¹ᐟ²x and A⁻¹ᐟ²x.

Here A = diag(1, √κ, κ). Squared coordinates are ((1 − q)(1 − p), q, (1 − q)p), so x is always normalized. At q = 0 and p = 1/2, equal mass on the two extreme eigenvalues attains the ceiling. An eigenvector gives P = 1. At κ = 1, every direction gives one.

Read the curve carefully

The green curve varies p while holding κ and q fixed. The dotted line is the bound for all directions, not necessarily an attainable value on that slice. The magenta point is your current direction. Increasing middle mass can keep this slice away from the sharp case.

In quadratic optimization, this inequality helps bound steepest-descent progress. This experiment isolates the algebraic product; it does not simulate training or predict a neural network’s convergence rate. ml-matrix computes the inverse and quadratic forms; the plots use floating-point arithmetic.
