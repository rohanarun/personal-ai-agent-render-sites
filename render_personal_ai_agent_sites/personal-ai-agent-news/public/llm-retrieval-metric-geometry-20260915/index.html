Three questions hiding behind “similar”

A dot product combines direction and magnitude: qᵀx = ||q|| ||x|| cos θ. A candidate can score highly because it is well aligned, long, or both. Cosine divides out both lengths and retains angular alignment. Squared Euclidean distance measures separation between endpoints: ||q−x||². Dot product and cosine are maximized; distance is minimized.

These are different objectives. Our small exact search evaluates all five candidates, so no approximate-index error can explain a ranking difference. The vectors are synthetic, not learned document representations. Three.js Vector3 operations calculate dot products, norms and distances; the table sorts their actual values.

Besides A and B, the fixed candidates are C=(0.1,0.4,0.6), D=(−0.5,0.2,0.4), E=(0.5,−0.5,−0.4). The B multiplier scales all its coordinates by a positive factor. The query rotates in the first two coordinates and has the selected positive length. No vector can become zero in this experiment.

An exact disagreement

At reset, A has dot product 0.8 and B has dot product 3. Dot product therefore chooses B. But A's cosine is 0.8/√0.68 ≈ 0.9701, while B's is 3/√10 ≈ 0.9487. Cosine chooses A: its angle is smaller despite its shorter length.

Squared distances tell a third story about the same pair. For A, (1−0.8)² + (0−0.2)² = 0.08. For B, (1−3)² + (0−1)² = 5. A is closer to this query endpoint. Cosine and L2 agree in this particular case, but their agreement is not a general identity on raw vectors.

To expose that difference, keep the angle at zero and increase query length to three. Now Q=(3,0,0). A's squared distance becomes 4.88 and B's becomes 1, so Euclidean retrieval switches to B. Cosine still prefers A because query direction did not change. Dot product still prefers B because multiplying a nonzero query by a positive scalar multiplies every score by the same factor.

Make length, not direction, change the result

Reset, choose dot product and reduce B's multiplier to 0.2. B becomes (0.6,0.2,0), with dot product 0.6. A now wins with 0.8. Switch to cosine and change the multiplier again: B's cosine remains 3/√10 throughout because multiplying by a positive scalar preserves its direction.

Euclidean distance need not move monotonically with this multiplier. A candidate on a fixed ray first approaches and then passes the point on that ray nearest the query. Expanding the expression gives ||q−cx||² = ||q||² − 2c qᵀx + c²||x||². When qᵀx is positive, its best unconstrained positive scale is c = qᵀx/||x||². For raw B and Q=(1,0,0), that scale is 0.3.

At c=0.3, B's squared distance is 0.1. A's remains 0.08, so even the best point along B's ray does not beat A here. This worked case separates an improvement in one candidate's score from a change in the overall winner.

Normalization creates a useful equivalence

Turn on normalization. Every query and candidate is replaced by its unit vector. The original directions remain; the original magnitudes are discarded. For unit vectors u and v:

||u−v||² = uᵀu + vᵀv − 2uᵀv = 2 − 2cos(u,v)

Now maximizing dot product, maximizing cosine and minimizing squared L2 produce the same ordering, apart from ties and numerical precision. The scores are not the same numbers: a cosine of 0.97 corresponds to a squared distance near 0.06. The table allows you to check the equation candidate by candidate.

Change either length control while normalization is enabled. Neither the transformed endpoints nor rankings should change. Turn normalization off and those length differences return. This is a change to the retrieval representation, not merely a cosmetic rescaling of the scene.

A metric decision is part of the retrieval design

Do not normalize by reflex. If an embedding model's intended scoring rule uses vector magnitude, normalization removes part of that rule. Conversely, treating raw inner product as cosine can silently introduce a length preference. The appropriate choice depends on the representation and task; this synthetic cloud does not establish which metric gives better document relevance.

Keep query and stored-candidate transformations consistent with the chosen scoring rule. When comparing two search configurations, distinguish a changed exact ranking objective from approximation error relative to that objective. This page performs exhaustive search over five points and has no index, latency measurements, recall benchmark or document labels.

Squared L2 and ordinary Euclidean distance have identical rankings because square root is strictly increasing on nonnegative values, but their thresholds differ. A squared-distance cutoff of 4 corresponds to a distance cutoff of 2. Similarly, the unit-vector identity can translate thresholds only under its normalization assumptions.

Zero vectors require a separate policy because their cosine is undefined mathematically. This experiment excludes them. Near ties can also depend on floating-point details; exact score ties here retain alphabetical order, which is a display rule rather than semantic evidence.
