Why the mean is right

For a sign vector z, each diagonal term contributes A[i,i] because z[i] squared is one. Every off-diagonal product has zero expectation because the signs are independent and centered. Thus E[z transpose A z] equals trace(A). Here the full distribution is small enough to enumerate rather than approximate.

Why the variance moves

For symmetric A, the random part is twice the sum over i less than j of A[i,j] z[i] z[j]. Distinct sign-pair products have zero cross expectation. The variance is therefore four times the sum of squared upper-triangular entries, equivalently twice the squared Frobenius norm of the off-diagonal part. The page compares this formula with the exact variance across all 16 probes.

A diagonal matrix has zero probe variance: every sign vector gives exactly its trace. Rotating the coordinates can introduce off-diagonal entries and variance while preserving every eigenvalue. This basis sensitivity belongs to the fixed-coordinate sign distribution, not to a changing trace.

Expectation is not a stopping rule

For N independent probes with replacement, the mean has variance equal to single-probe variance divided by N. Its theoretical standard deviation is not the realized error, nor is one standard deviation a certified confidence interval. Sampling all 16 sign vectors exactly once would give the exact trace, but a random batch of 16 can contain repeats and omissions.

Why ML systems use probes

In large models, a matrix may be available through matrix-vector products while forming every entry is expensive. Randomized trace estimation trades those products for statistical error. This tiny explicit matrix exposes the mechanism; it does not benchmark a neural-network Hessian or claim that its positive-definite spectrum represents every training problem.

Roosta-Khorasani and Ascher: Improved bounds on sample size for implicit matrix trace estimators describes the Rademacher estimator and distinguishes variance comparisons from probabilistic accuracy guarantees.
