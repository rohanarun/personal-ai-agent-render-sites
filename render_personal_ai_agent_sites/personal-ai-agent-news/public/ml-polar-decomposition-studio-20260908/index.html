Two operations, one map

For a real square matrix A, a polar decomposition writes A = U H. The matrix U is orthogonal: U^T U = I. The matrix H is symmetric positive semidefinite and is the unique positive semidefinite square root of A^T A. Applied to a column vector, H acts first, then U. The order matters.

The stretch has no negative eigenvalues. Its principal stretch factors are the singular values of A. The orthogonal part preserves lengths and angles; it may be a reflection rather than a rotation. For a nonsingular A, U is unique and its determinant has the same sign as the determinant of A.

What collapse leaves undecided

A singular map erases at least one direction. Its stretch H is still unique, but an orthogonal U need not be. This studio displays one valid orthogonal completion chosen by the SVD engine, not a uniquely determined turn. The zero-map preset makes the ambiguity especially clear: any orthogonal U multiplied by H = 0 yields the same zero map.

The displayed U is also a closest orthogonal matrix to A in Frobenius norm. Its distance is the square root of the sum of (singular value - 1) squared. This does not constrain U to a proper rotation. Reflection-free alignment is a different optimization problem.

The polygon is a fixed diagnostic shape, not training data. Four matrix entries range from -2 to 2. Numerical factors come from ml-matrix SVD: if A = P S V^T, then U = P V^T and H = V S V^T. The reconstruction and orthogonality residuals expose floating-point error; rank is classified with a 10^-10 tolerance.
