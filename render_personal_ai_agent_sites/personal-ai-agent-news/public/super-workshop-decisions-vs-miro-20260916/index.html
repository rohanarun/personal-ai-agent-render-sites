Votes are not decisions

How to turn a workshop board into a decision brief without inventing consensus.

A board full of sticky notes can capture a productive conversation and still leave the next step unclear. The missing ingredient is not necessarily another summarizer. It is a record of who could decide, what they decided, and who accepted the resulting work.

Keep four kinds of evidence separate

An idea is a possibility. A vote is a preference recorded under a particular voting setup. A decision is a choice made under the team's agreed authority or rule. An action is work that somebody has accepted with a clear completion condition. Summarizing all four as “the team agreed” destroys useful information.

Consider a fictional onboarding workshop. “Rewrite the setup guide” receives six dots; “add a video walkthrough” receives four. Those numbers alone do not reveal how many people voted, whether each person had multiple dots, whether anyone abstained, or whether a product lead retained the final decision. Six dots cannot establish six supporters, unanimity, or permission to spend money.

Before the session ends, capture the decision rule, the actual choice, unresolved objections, and a review date. Ask the proposed owner to accept a bounded task. “Draft a revised first-run checklist for review” is different from “ship a new onboarding experience.” Keep the original board reference beside the brief so someone can challenge a mistaken summary.

Actual cache technology, limited claims
The public computer-use-cache repository provides inspectable implementation, not merely a marketing label. In the pinned server source, normalized requests are hashed to lookup stored responses. Eligible successful JSON responses can be reused; misses go upstream, with expiry and bypass controls.
A matching cached model response can avoid that upstream model call. It does not prove that a board is current, a person accepted ownership, or a browser action succeeded. Do not reuse yesterday's decision as today's approval. This public implementation is evidence of cache technology, not proof of every deployed product setting or measured savings in this workshop workflow.


A useful trial has an acceptance test
Start with one non-sensitive workshop and preserve its original notes. Have a facilitator compare the proposed brief against the record: every decision must have authority, every accepted task must have an owner response, and every unresolved objection must remain visible. Mark contradictions for review. Do not send the brief as an official record until the responsible people approve it.
Measure correction time and missing evidence in that trial, rather than assuming a time-saving percentage. Keep personal or confidential material out unless the team's permissions and data policies allow processing. If the draft repeatedly upgrades suggestions into commitments, stop automatic distribution and revise the instructions before trying again.
