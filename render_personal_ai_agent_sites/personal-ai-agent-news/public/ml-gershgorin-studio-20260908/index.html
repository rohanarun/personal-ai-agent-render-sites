From rows to regions

For each row, Gershgorin's theorem forms a disc centered at its diagonal entry, with radius equal to the sum of absolute off-diagonal entries. Every eigenvalue belongs to the union of these discs. Our matrix is real and symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real: the chart shows the discs' intersections with the real axis.

If every interval lies strictly to the right of zero, all eigenvalues are positive. This certifies positive definiteness. An interval crossing zero does not prove a negative eigenvalue. Nor must each overlapping interval contain its own distinct eigenvalue.

Shift the matrix, not the coupling

Adding t times the identity increases every diagonal entry and eigenvalue by t while leaving the row radii unchanged. The displayed strict shift threshold is derived from the unshifted lower bound. Equality is not a positive-definite certificate; the lowest possible eigenvalue could be zero.

In a quadratic model q(v) = v-transpose A v / 2, positive definiteness means positive curvature in every nonzero direction. Diagonal damping is one way to alter that model. This demonstration does not claim that damping alone guarantees convergence of a nonlinear machine-learning optimizer.

Compare sufficient with necessary

The second preset has positive numerical eigenvalues even though its first interval reaches below zero. It illustrates the gap between a sufficient bound and a complete spectral test. The negative-curvature preset has a genuinely negative eigenvalue; shifting far enough moves it above zero.

The library ml-matrix computes the numerical eigenvalues independently of the row bounds. Values within 1e-9 of zero are reported as near zero, not as proof of exact singularity. The certificate also uses a 1e-9 numerical margin. Controls keep the matrix symmetric and bounded; this is a three-variable illustration, not a large-Hessian benchmarking claim.
