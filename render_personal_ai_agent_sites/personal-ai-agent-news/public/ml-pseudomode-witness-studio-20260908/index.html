Make a target an eigenvalue

The eigenvalues stay at -1 and -2. Yet an off-diagonal coupling can make other real targets surprisingly cheap to reach with a matrix perturbation. The smallest singular value tells us the price, and its vectors construct a witness.

A concrete witness
Unit pseudomode v
Before correction, Av misses zv by the minimum amount. The witness is not necessarily unique; singular-vector signs may change together.
Correction E
This rank-at-most-one correction is allowed to change every entry. It does not preserve triangular structure or a particular model parameterization.
A residual becomes an eigenpair
Let B = zI - A. A smallest singular triplet satisfies Bv = s u with unit vectors u and v. Set E = s u v transpose. Then Ev = s u = Bv, so (A + E)v = zv and the spectral norm of E is s. No smaller correction can do this for any unit vector: the smallest singular value bounds the residual from below.
The closed epsilon-pseudospectrum contains targets whose minimum correction is at most epsilon. This display shows only its real-axis slice, not the full complex-plane region. When z is already an eigenvalue, the correction is zero and the resolvent is singular, conventionally assigned infinite norm.
What the eigenvalues leave out
For k = 0, the required correction equals the distance to the nearest eigenvalue. With coupling, the matrix is nonnormal and that distance can overstate robustness. In learned linear dynamics or numerical model analysis, this is a warning to inspect perturbation behavior as well as eigenvalues. These are synthetic matrices, not a measured model or a stability certificate for a nonlinear system.
