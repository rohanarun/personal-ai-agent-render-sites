Why averaging equivalent neural networks can fail

Two networks make exactly the same predictions. Their average makes different ones. The missing ingredient is the identity of each hidden neuron.

Learn to distinguish a function from its parameterization, recognize hidden-unit permutation symmetry, and explain why alignment matters before merging weights.

Three experiments worth doing

  1. Compare the endpoints. Move t from 0 to 1 with neurons left swapped. The parameters change sign, but the prediction curve and loss return to their starting values. This is a symmetry, not two different learned behaviors.
  2. Inspect t = 0.50. Both output weights become zero. The network loses its varying response and outputs −0.7 for every input. At x = 0.5, the target is 0.5, the prediction is −0.7 and the squared error is 1.44. The displayed mean loss averages across all 201 inputs, not just this probe.
  3. Align, then repeat. Select “Align B to A.” In this deliberately exact example, undoing the swap makes the parameter vectors identical. Every interpolation point now stays at A with zero loss. The old gold path remains visible as a comparison.

Averaging weights is not averaging predictions. An ensemble that averages A's and B's outputs would remain exactly correct here, because those outputs are already identical. It requires both forward evaluations rather than producing this single averaged parameter vector.

Derive the symmetry, then the failure

Let R(z) = max(0, z), the ReLU activation. Both hidden input weights are fixed at 1. The two hidden biases are d and −d; their output weights are a and −a. The output bias is fixed at −0.7.

f(x; d, a) = a R(x + d) − a R(x − d) − 0.7
A: d = 0.7, a = 1
B: d = −0.7, a = −1

Replacing (d, a) with (−d, −a) exchanges the two summands. Their sum is unchanged for every real x. More generally, permuting a hidden layer's units must also permute the corresponding incoming weights, biases and outgoing connections consistently. A neuron slot number is not a semantic label.

Naive parameter interpolation gives d(t) = 0.7(1 − 2t) and a(t) = 1 − 2t. At t = 1/2, d = a = 0. Substitution leaves only the output bias. The nonlinearity means a straight line in parameter space need not preserve the function.

L(d, a) = (1 / 201) Σᵢ [f(xᵢ; d, a) − f(xᵢ; 0.7, 1)]²
xᵢ = −2 + 4i / 200, i = 0, …, 200

The 3D surface evaluates this expression over d ∈ [−0.9, 0.9] and a ∈ [−1.2, 1.2]. Its two axes are a tied two-parameter family, not all parameters of a general network. Surface height and color encode the same loss; the numeric readout is authoritative.

What this means for real model merging

Independently trained networks can assign different features to corresponding hidden-unit slots. A raw weight average can then mix incompatible coordinates. The Git Re-Basin paper by Ainsworth, Hayase and Srinivasa studies permutation alignment before merging and reports low-barrier connections in several evaluated settings. It also discusses limitations and a counterexample; it is not a universal merging guarantee.

Our alignment is known in advance because we constructed B by swapping A. Real models may differ beyond a permutation. Finding a useful correspondence can require a matching objective and an algorithm, and residual connections or other architecture constraints must be respected. You still need evaluation on the intended tasks and data.

What this experiment cannot establish

It does not train networks, reproduce the paper's experiments, show that every pair of minima is connected without a barrier, or predict whether two LLM checkpoints will merge well. It uses noiseless targets from A itself and a finite one-dimensional input grid. Zero measured loss is expected at the endpoints, not evidence of generalization on a real dataset.

Practical takeaway: check parameter correspondence before interpreting a weight interpolation. Compare it with output ensembling, and separate the benefit of alignment from any claim about downstream quality.
